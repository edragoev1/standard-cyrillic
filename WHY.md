# Why Standard Cyrillic

## The letters

Bulgarian children learn to write Cyrillic by hand in a joined script, in
which т is written as an m-shape, д loops below the line like a g, и is
written like a u and п like an n. In print, the same letters had their
standard shapes: т like a small capital T, д with its two feet, и and п
as mirror and plain forms of the capital letters.

Over the last decade, more and more Bulgarian websites, newspapers among
them, draw the handwritten shapes in upright print. Some fonts switch to
them when text is marked as Bulgarian (OpenType's `locl` feature, which
browsers turn on for `lang="bg"`); some, such as a newspaper's own fonts,
draw them always. A reader who has read Bulgarian in the standard shapes
all their life meets a page of m's, g's and u's that are not m, g and u.

## What research says

There is no study, that we know of, of these Bulgarian shapes and how fast
they are read. There is a close case, studied for years: Serbian, written
in both Cyrillic and Latin letters, by the same readers.

In the late 1970s and the 1980s, Georgije Lukatela, Michael Turvey, Laurie
Feldman and their colleagues compared how quickly Serbian readers
recognized words made of letters that exist in both alphabets but sound
different in each, such as В, Н, Р and С (Р is "r" in Cyrillic and "p" in
Latin), with words made of letters that belong to one alphabet only.
Skilled adult readers were often slower, and made more errors, on the
words of ambiguous letters: the "phonological ambiguity effect", whose
size depends, among other things, on whether the letters make a word in
one of the alphabets. The reader does not
settle on one alphabet for the page; each ambiguous letter keeps both of
its readings in play for a moment. Among the papers:

- G. Lukatela, M. Savić, B. Gligorijević, P. Ognjenović and M. T. Turvey,
  "Bi-alphabetical lexical decision", *Language and Speech* 21 (1978),
  142–165.
- L. B. Feldman and M. T. Turvey, "Visual word recognition in Serbo-Croatian
  is phonologically analytic", *Journal of Experimental Psychology: Human
  Perception and Performance* 9 (1983), 288–298.

Teachers of English to Russian and Bulgarian speakers know the same thing
from the other side: the first errors are the "false friend" letters, p
read as r, H as n, C as s, and they fade with practice.

## Our own thinking

The standard shapes of т, д, г, и and п look like no Latin letter. The
handwritten ones look like m, g, s, u and n. So these shapes add letters
that read one way in Bulgarian and another in English, exactly where the
Serbian studies found a cost.

For a fluent adult reading a Bulgarian page, the cost is small: the
studies measured milliseconds per word, and the context sorts most of it
out. It is felt as friction more than as lost meaning. It matters more
where the two alphabets meet:

- children learning to read Bulgarian and English at the same time;
- learners of English, for whom the false-friend letters are already a
  hurdle;
- mixed text, which is how most people read online: Bulgarian sentences
  with English names, brands, web addresses and code in them.

English opens more doors for a young Bulgarian than any other language,
and anything that adds friction between the two alphabets works against
that. Print can keep the two alphabets apart where it is able to, and the
standard shapes do. Handwriting and italics can keep their own shapes, as
they always have.

There is a fair case on the other side: Bulgarians learn the handwritten
shapes at school, Russian italics use the same shapes, and readers switch
between them by context every day. We do not claim the new shapes are
proven harder to read; we know of no study either way. We do find them
harder to read, page after page, and the research on letters shared by
two alphabets says why that is to be expected.

## So, an extension

Publishers will choose their own fonts, and we do not ask them to change.
This changes only what the reader sees on their own screen: the standard
shapes, in the same weight and size, on any site, with nothing collected
and nothing sent anywhere. It started as a tool for one reader's own
reading of Bulgarian magazines, and it is free for anyone who reads the
same way.
